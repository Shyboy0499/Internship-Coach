## 1. The baseline: what every intern is expected to do

Meeting these consistently earns a "solid intern" review. They aren't glamorous, but everything else is built on them. An intern who takes on stretch projects while missing the baseline doesn't stand out — they worry their mentor.

- **Reliable.** On time, meets commitments or flags early, reachable during work hours.
- **Communicates.** Regular updates, well-formed questions, no surprises.
- **Learns.** Doesn't need the same thing explained twice; more independent each week.
- **Delivers the assigned project** to the agreed scope and a reasonable quality bar.
- **Takes feedback** and applies it without getting defensive.
- **Professional.** Respectful, follows company policies (security, confidentiality, rules on AI tools), good meeting manners.
- **Engaged.** Curious, takes part in team meetings, cares how things turn out.

## 2. Expectations by stage of the internship

For a typical 10–12 week internship:

- **Weeks 1–2: ramp up.** You're expected to ask lots of questions, get set up, learn names and tools, and land a small first contribution. Nobody expects real output yet.
- **Weeks 3–6: contribute with guidance.** Steady progress on your project with regular check-ins. Your questions get more specific, and you start catching your own mistakes.
- **Week 7 to the end: increasing independence.** You drive your scoped project with light guidance, propose next steps instead of waiting to be told, then finish, document, and present.

On a longer co-op, the bar by the end is close to a junior team member working on well-scoped tasks.

## 3. Meets vs. stands out: a self-check

Useful for a midpoint self-review, or when an intern asks "am I doing well?"

| Area | Meets expectations | Stands out |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | Finishes the assigned project | Finishes it with docs, tests, and a handoff — and it actually gets used |
| Ownership | Does the tasks they're given | Drives the project to done: chases loose ends, unblocks themselves, anticipates the next step |
| Communication | Gives updates when asked | Updates proactively; flags risks with options; writes clearly enough that the mentor can forward it to their own manager |
| Problem solving | Asks for help after trying | Brings a proposed answer with the question: "I think we should do A, because…" |
| Learning | Learns the tools the task needs | Learns the business and the *why*, picks up adjacent skills, and teaches others what they learned |
| Quality | Work is correct after review | Self-reviews first, thinks through edge cases; reviews find little |
| Feedback | Applies the specific feedback | Asks for feedback unprompted, and applies it across all their work, not just the one instance |
| Initiative | Asks for more work when done | Spots useful problems and proposes small fixes — then does them once approved |
| Team | Gets along with the team | Builds relationships beyond the mentor, helps other interns, makes the team better |
| Visibility | The mentor knows what they did | The team knows what they did — through a demo, a write-up, or the final presentation |

## 4. How to stand out

1. **Nail the baseline first.** Everything below only counts on top of it.
2. **Understand the why.** Find out who uses your work and what goal it serves; ask "How will this be used?" Interns who connect their task to the team's goals make better decisions and ask better questions, and mentors notice immediately.
3. **Own it end to end.** "It works on my machine" isn't done. Done means tests, docs, edge cases, a handoff, and a follow-up after it ships — someone else can use it without you. See `delivering-work.md` for the full checklist.
4. **Bring solutions, not just problems.** Pair every question or issue with a proposal. Even when the proposal is wrong, it shows judgment and makes the mentor's job easier.
5. **Make your mentor's life easier.** Updates they can forward as-is, notes from meetings you both attend, small chores taken off their plate.
6. **Leave things better than you found them.** Fix the onboarding doc you struggled with, write a setup guide for the next intern, automate a repetitive task (with permission). Low risk, highly visible, appreciated by everyone.
7. **Measure your impact.** Capture before-and-after numbers — time saved, errors reduced, people reached — in your work log. They make the final presentation and your resume far stronger.
8. **Share what you learn.** A short write-up or demo at a team meeting, or an answer to another intern's question in the team channel. Teaching shows mastery.
9. **Build relationships beyond your mentor.** Return-offer decisions usually involve more people than your mentor. Coffee chats across the team, and with neighboring teams, help.
10. **Ask for a stretch** once your core project is clearly on track: "I have some capacity — is there something more challenging I could take on?"
11. **Ask what a clear yes looks like.** Around the midpoint, ask the manager directly: "What would make a return offer a clear yes?" Most interns never ask; the answer tells you exactly what to aim at. More in `return-offer.md`.
12. **Be good to work with.** Curious, positive, humble, dependable. Attitude counts for more than most interns expect.

## 5. Skills beyond the role: knowing how to do more

Interns who stand out can usually do a bit more than their job description — enough to unblock themselves and to help outside their own lane. Encourage the intern to pick **one or two** of these to grow during the internship rather than chasing all of them.

**Useful in almost any role:**

- **Clear writing.** A one-page proposal or summary a busy person can read in three minutes. The most underrated skill in any workplace.
- **Presenting and demoing.** Telling the story of your work, not just listing what you did.
- **Data literacy.** Enough spreadsheet skill and basic SQL to answer your own questions with data instead of waiting for someone else.
- **Light automation.** A little scripting — Python, Google Apps Script, spreadsheet macros — to remove repetitive work.
- **Using AI tools well.** Speeding up research, drafting, and coding while checking the output and following company policy.
- **Business sense.** How the company makes money, which metrics leadership watches, who the customers are.
- **Project management basics.** Breaking work into milestones, estimating, tracking progress, running a short meeting.

For role-specific extras, see the **To stand out** line in each section of `role-skills.md`.

## 6. Standing out without backfiring

- **Baseline first.** Extra work never makes up for the assigned project. A stretch project while the main one slips reads as poor judgment.
- **Check before going beyond scope.** Propose it, get a yes, then do it.
- **Don't step on toes.** Don't "fix" another team's work without talking to them first.
- **Visibility isn't self-promotion.** Share your work, and credit the people who helped.
- **Sustainable beats heroic.** Late nights to look impressive usually signal poor prioritization, and they lead to burnout. Steady, consistent effort is what mentors remember.
- **Quality over quantity.** One finished, polished, used piece of work beats five half-done ones.
