## 1. What mentors actually expect

Most mentors carry a full workload of their own; the intern is on top of it. What they hope for:

- **Reliability.** You do what you said, by when you said — or tell them early that you can't.
- **Visible learning.** You're noticeably more independent in week 6 than in week 1.
- **Low-friction communication.** Your questions and updates are quick to read and easy to answer.
- **Good judgment about when to ask.** Not never, not constantly.
- **Engagement.** You care about the work and the team, not only about the line on your resume.

What they do *not* expect: that you already know their codebase, tools, or jargon; that you're fast at first; or that you never make mistakes.

A reframe that helps anxious interns: the mentor's own performance is partly judged by how their intern does. They want you to succeed, and the habits below make that easy for them.

## 2. What frustrates mentors, and the fix

| Behavior | Why it lands badly | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Stuck for hours or days, says nothing | They find out at the deadline, about a problem they could have solved in five minutes | Timebox, then ask; mention blockers in every update |
| "It doesn't work" / "Can you help?" with no detail | They have to interrogate you before they can help | Goal, what you tried, what happened, your guess |
| Asking without trying anything | Feels like outsourcing your thinking to them | Make a bounded attempt first and say what you tried |
| Asking the same question twice | Signals their first answer wasn't valued | Keep a notes doc and search it before asking |
| Sending "hi" and waiting for a reply | They have to wait on you just to find out what you need | Put the whole question in the first message |
| Saying "got it" without getting it | You build the wrong thing | Repeat the task back; ask the "obvious" question now |
| Missing a deadline without warning | Breaks trust, and can blindside them with their own boss | Flag early with a new estimate and a plan |
| Hiding or downplaying a mistake | A small problem grows; trust takes the bigger hit | Report quickly, with impact and a fix |
| Needing the same feedback repeatedly | Feels like talking to a wall | Write feedback down; show you applied it |
| Submitting work you can't explain (including AI-generated work) | They can't trust it, and review takes longer | Understand and test it before you hand it over |
| Dropping one huge change at the end | Hard to review, risky, and too late to redirect | Deliver small pieces; check direction early |
| Turning up to 1:1s with nothing prepared | Wastes their time | Bring a short agenda |
| Late, absent, or unreachable during work hours | Feels unreliable | Be on time; say when you'll be away |
| Expanding scope or rewriting things unasked | Creates review burden and risk | Propose first, then do it if they agree |
| Treating the mentor as the only resource | Makes them a bottleneck | Use docs, search, teammates, and other interns too |

## 3. Asking good questions

### When to ask

- **Ordinary technical or research problem:** about 30–60 minutes of real effort first.
- **Blocked by something you can't fix yourself** (access, credentials, missing data, a decision that isn't yours): ask right away.
- **Anything that could cause harm** (deleting data, touching production systems, sending something outside the company, legal or compliance doubts): ask *before* acting, every time.
- **First week or two:** ask a little sooner. Ramp-up questions are expected.

### The question formula

1. **Goal:** what you're trying to do, and why.
2. **What you tried,** and what you've ruled out.
3. **What happened:** the exact error, output, or point of confusion. Paste it or screenshot it.
4. **Your best guess** or proposed next step.
5. **Urgency:** whether it's blocking you.

Ending with a guess turns the mentor's job from "figure this out from scratch" into "yes, or no — try this instead." It also shows them how you think.

### Where and how to ask

- If the team has a help channel, ask there unless it's sensitive: answers come faster and help others. Some mentors prefer DMs — ask them once which they want.
- Batch non-urgent questions into one message or a list for your next 1:1.
- Never send "hi" or "are you there?" and wait. Send the full question in the first message.
- Watch out for the "XY problem": ask about what you're actually trying to achieve, not only about the workaround you got stuck on.
- Once you're unblocked, now and then ask "How would you have figured that out?" It teaches you to help yourself next time, and mentors love the question.

## 4. The getting-unstuck ladder

Climb in order, without camping on any one rung:

1. **Re-read** the task, the error message, and the relevant docs slowly. A surprising share of problems are answered right there.
2. **Search** the internal wiki and docs, the codebase or shared drive, past tickets, team channel history — then the web.
3. **Shrink the problem.** Reproduce it in the smallest possible case. Change one thing at a time.
4. **Explain it in writing.** Drafting the question to your mentor often solves it ("rubber-ducking").
5. **Ask a peer:** another intern, a teammate, an onboarding buddy.
6. **Ask your mentor,** using the formula above.

When you get the answer, write it in your notes doc.

## 5. Status updates and flagging risks

**Cadence.** Agree on one with your mentor. A sensible default: a short end-of-day message for the first few weeks, then a weekly summary once you're settled.

**Format.** Done / Next / Blocked (or risks). A few lines. Lead with anything they need to act on.

**Flagging a risk.** Speak up as soon as you suspect an estimate will slip — not at the deadline. Say what's slipping, why, your new estimate, and the options (cut scope, extend, get help). With notice, a mentor can adjust plans; with a surprise, they can't.

**Estimating.** Interns almost always underestimate. For unfamiliar work, roughly double your first instinct, and say how confident you are.

## 6. Making 1:1s useful

Keep a shared running doc so the history builds up, and bring a short agenda:

- Progress since last time (one or two lines)
- Blockers, or decisions you need from them
- Batched questions
- One specific feedback ask
- Occasionally, a career or learning question

**Good questions to ask a mentor over the internship:**

- "What does a great internship look like from your side?"
- "How do you prefer I reach you — chat, email, or saving things for our 1:1?"
- "What's one thing I should do differently?"
- "Is the way I ask questions working for you?"
- "If you were me, what would you focus on learning?"
- "Who else on the team should I talk to?"
- At the midpoint: "Am I on track for a return offer? What would make it a clear yes?"

**After the 1:1:** write down the action items, do them, and report back.

## 7. Feedback: getting it and using it

**Ask for it specifically.** "Was my analysis doc at the right level of detail for your manager?" gets a far better answer than "Any feedback?" Ask early — around week 2 or 3 — not only at the midpoint.

**Receive it well:**

1. Thank them. Don't defend or explain yet — listen.
2. If it's vague, ask: "Could you give me an example?" or "What would better have looked like?"
3. Write it down.
4. Apply it, then close the loop: "I tried your suggestion on the last PR — split it into three smaller ones."

**If you disagree,** make sure you understand their point first, then share your reasoning once, briefly and with curiosity. On their team's work, accept their call — they usually have context you don't.

Critical feedback stings; that's normal. Feedback means they're investing in you. The interns who get no feedback are usually the ones mentors have given up on.

## 8. Mistakes

Everyone breaks something eventually. What mentors judge is speed and ownership.

1. **Contain it** if it's still causing damage — but if it involves a live or shared system, don't attempt frantic fixes on your own; ask.
2. **Tell your mentor quickly** — within the hour for anything that affects other people.
3. **Say:** what happened, the impact, what you've already done, and what you propose.
4. **One clear apology,** then ownership and a plan. Don't over-apologize, and don't blame tools or other people.
5. **Follow through** on the prevention step, and mention later that you did.

## 9. Workload: too little, too much, unclear

**Too little work.** Say so — busy mentors often don't notice. Come with a proposal: "I've finished X. I could do Y or Z next, or is there something more useful?" In the meantime: fix the docs you found confusing, add tests, read code or papers related to your project, and ask to sit in on meetings.

**Too much, or conflicting priorities.** Don't quietly work late to hide it. Ask: "I have A, B, and C on my plate and I don't think I can finish all three by Friday. Which matters most?"

**Unclear task.** Repeat it back, write a short plan, and get a thumbs-up before going deep.

## 10. Reading and adapting to your mentor

Every mentor works differently. Notice:

- **How they write.** Short chat replies usually mean they prefer short messages; long written docs mean they value written detail.
- **When they're available.** Focus hours, meeting-heavy days, time zones.
- **Signs of overload.** Terse replies, rescheduled 1:1s. Usually not about you. Batch questions, lean on other resources, keep updates crisp.

If they're unsure whether their mentor is annoyed, the intern can simply ask in a 1:1: "Is the way I'm working with you working for you, or would you prefer something different?" Most mentors appreciate the question.

**If the mentor is rarely available:** lock in a fixed recurring 1:1, find a second point of contact (onboarding buddy or teammate), lean harder on docs, and if it's blocking progress for more than a week or so, tell the manager or program coordinator — framed as asking for help, not complaining.

## 11. When the relationship isn't working

Sometimes styles just clash.

1. **Self-check first.** Is there feedback you haven't acted on? A habit from section 2 that applies?
2. **Have a direct, low-drama conversation.** "I want to make sure I'm being useful to you. What could I be doing differently?"
3. **If it persists,** talk to the program coordinator, the mentor's manager, or your recruiter, framed as wanting to succeed: "I'd like some advice on working more effectively with my mentor."

## 12. When it's not yours to fix

Everything above is about being a good intern. This section is the exception. If the intern describes:

- harassment, discriminatory comments, or unwanted personal attention
- pressure to do something unsafe, unethical, or illegal (for example falsifying data, or skipping safety or security rules)
- consistently demeaning treatment — yelling, public humiliation, insults
- unpaid overtime, or work that seems to break labor rules or their internship agreement
- retaliation for raising a concern

…then "don't upset the mentor" is the wrong goal. Help them:

- **Record what happened:** dates, specifics, and copies of messages, kept where they're allowed to keep them.
- **Find the right person to tell:** HR, the internship program or recruiting coordinator, the mentor's manager, their university's career or internship office (especially for internships taken for credit), or a company ethics or reporting line.
- **Hear clearly** that it isn't their fault, and that raising it is legitimate.

Don't play lawyer. For legal questions, point them to their university office or local labor authority. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.
