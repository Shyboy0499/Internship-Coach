# Presenting Your Work

Midpoint check-ins, team demos, intern showcases, and the final presentation often decide how an internship is remembered. Most of the skill is in the preparation.

## 1. Know the room

Before building anything, find out:

- **Who's there** — your team, your mentor's manager, leadership, other interns? Leaders want the answer and the impact; engineers want how it works.
- **How long** — and plan for about 80% of it, so there's room for questions.
- **The format** — slides, a live demo, a document, or a mix; in person or remote; recorded or not.
- **What success looks like** — ask your mentor: "What would you want people to take away from my presentation?"

## 2. Tell a story, not a task list

Most intern presentations list everything they did, week by week. Strong ones tell a story:

1. **The problem** — what was wrong or missing, in one or two sentences, from the user's or the business's point of view.
2. **Why it matters** — who it affects, and how much.
3. **What you did** — the approach and the two or three most important decisions, not every step.
4. **The result** — show it (a demo, a before-and-after, a number). Be honest about size: "Saves the support team about 3 hours a week" beats a vague "big impact".
5. **What's next** — what's left, what you'd do with more time, where it lives now.
6. **Thanks** — name the people who helped.

For leadership audiences, lead with the result ("We cut report prep from 3 hours to 10 minutes"), then explain how.

## 3. Slides that work

- **One idea per slide**, with an **action title** that states the point: "Exports cut support tickets by 20%" rather than "Results".
- **Show, don't list:** screenshots, a short demo, one clear chart, a before-and-after.
- **Numbers need context:** compared with what, over what period, measured how.
- **Large text, few words.** If people are reading, they're not listening.
- **Respect confidentiality:** if slides will travel outside the team, check what can be shared.
- **Have a backup** of any live demo — a recording or screenshots — in case the network or the environment fails.

## 4. Rehearse

- **Out loud, at least twice**, with a timer. Silent read-throughs hide the stumbles.
- **Do a dry run with your mentor** or a friendly teammate, and ask: "What's unclear? What would you cut?"
- **Cut ruthlessly** to fit the time. Running over is the most common mistake, and it eats the question time.
- **Script the first 30 seconds** word for word. Once you're through the opening, nerves usually settle.
- **Test the setup** beforehand: screen sharing, demo data, the projector or video call.

## 5. Handling questions

- **Prepare for the five most likely questions** — the hardest ones especially ("Why didn't you use X?", "How does this scale?", "What would you do differently?").
- **Listen to the whole question**, and repeat or rephrase it if it's unclear. It buys thinking time and makes sure you answer the right thing.
- **Answer briefly first**, then offer more: "Short answer: yes, it handles that. Happy to go into how afterwards."
- **"I don't know — I'll find out and follow up" is a strong answer.** Bluffing is the weak one. Then actually follow up.
- **Hand off when it fits:** "My mentor knows the history there better than me — Sam?"
- **Park off-topic or long questions:** "Great question — can we take it offline after?"

## 6. Nerves

Nerves are normal and rarely as visible as they feel. Prepare more than you think you need, slow down (nervous speakers rush), pause after key points, keep water nearby, and remember the audience wants you to do well — most of them gave an awkward first presentation once too.

## 7. Afterwards

- Send the slides or recording to the people who attended, with a thank-you.
- Follow up on every "I'll find out" within a day or two.
- Note the feedback and questions in your work log or notebook — they make good material for the self-review and resume.

## 8. Outline templates

**5-minute update or demo**

```
0:00  The problem, in one sentence
0:30  What I built — demo or screenshots
3:00  The result, and one edge case it handles
4:00  What's next, and what I need
4:30  Questions
```

**10–15 minute final presentation**

```
1. Who I am, and the problem I worked on (1 min)
2. Why it matters — who it affects, and how much (1–2 min)
3. What I did — the approach and two or three key decisions (3–4 min)
4. The result — demo, before-and-after, numbers (2–3 min)
5. What I learned (1 min)
6. What's next and where it lives (1 min)
7. Thank-yous (30 sec)
8. Questions (the remaining time)
```

**Q&A prep list**

```
Likely question → short answer → where to find more detail
1. ...
2. ...
3. ...
4. ...
5. ...
Questions I can't answer yet → who to ask before the presentation
```
