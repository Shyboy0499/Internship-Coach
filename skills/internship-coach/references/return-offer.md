# Return Offers, References, and Leaving Well

How return-offer decisions usually get made, how to ask about one, what to do whatever the answer is, and how to leave with references and relationships intact.

## 1. How return-offer decisions are usually made

Processes vary by company, but most decisions combine:

- **Your mentor's and manager's assessment** — usually against the baseline expectations, plus how much you stood out (see `expectations.md`).
- **Feedback from others** you worked with, and sometimes your final presentation.
- **Headcount and budget** — often the biggest factor, and outside your control. Many strong interns don't get offers simply because there's no open role.
- **Timing** — some companies decide before the internship ends, others weeks later.

Ask your recruiter or program coordinator early how the process works and when decisions are made. It's a normal question.

## 2. Asking about a return offer

- **Ask around the midpoint**, not in the last week — there's still time to act on the answer.
- **Be direct and specific:** "I'd love to come back. What would make a return offer a clear yes? Is there anything you'd want to see from me in the second half?"
- **Ask about the process,** not just the outcome: who decides, when, and whether there's a formal review.
- **Act visibly** on whatever they tell you, and mention it in later updates.
- **Make your interest known to more than your mentor** — your manager and the recruiter or program coordinator too.

## 3. If you get an offer

- **Say thank you** — warmly, to your mentor and manager.
- **Ask for the details in writing** and the decision deadline.
- **It's normal to take time to decide,** and to ask questions: the team, the role, start date, location, and any part of the compensation you don't understand.
- **If you're weighing other offers,** say so honestly and ask whether there's flexibility on the deadline. Your university's career office can help you compare offers and think through negotiation.

## 4. If you don't get an offer

It hurts, and it's often about headcount, not you. Acknowledge the disappointment before anything else. Then:

- **Finish strong anyway.** People remember how you handled the last two weeks, and the same people may hire, refer, or recommend you later.
- **Ask for feedback:** "What would have made me a stronger candidate? What should I work on next?"
- **Ask for a reference** from your mentor or manager while your work is fresh.
- **Ask whether they'd refer you** to other teams or keep you in mind if a role opens.
- **Leave on good terms** — thank-yous, a clean handoff, LinkedIn connections.

## 5. If you don't want to come back

- **Decline graciously and promptly,** so the spot can go to someone else.
- **Thank them specifically** for what you learned.
- **Keep the door open:** "I'd love to stay in touch, and I hope our paths cross again."
- **Still ask for a reference** if the internship went well.

## 6. References and recommendations

- **Who:** your mentor and your manager first; anyone else who saw your work closely.
- **When:** before you leave, while your work is fresh in their memory — ask in person or by message, then follow up in writing.
- **How:** ask whether they'd be *comfortable* being a reference — it gives them an easy way to decline.
- **Make it easy for them:** send a short summary of what you worked on, your results, and the kinds of roles you're applying for. Your work log or notebook is the source.
- **LinkedIn recommendation:** ask once, offer a few points they could mention, and never pressure.
- **Keep them updated** when you use them as a reference, and tell them how it turned out.

## 7. Leaving well and staying in touch

- **Hand off cleanly** — a handoff doc, open tasks reassigned, access returned (see `timeline.md`).
- **Thank people specifically** — what they did and what it meant.
- **Connect on LinkedIn** with the people you worked with.
- **Send a short update a few months later** — what you're doing now, and a thank-you for something specific. It keeps the relationship warm for references, referrals, and future roles.

## 8. Message templates

**Asking about a return offer**

```
I've really enjoyed working on [project] with the team, and I'd love to come back after [graduation / my next term]. Could we talk about how return offers work here and whether I'm on track? Is there anything you'd want to see from me in the remaining weeks?
```

**Asking for feedback after no offer**

```
Thanks for letting me know, and for being straightforward about it. I've learned a lot here. Would you be willing to share what would have made me a stronger candidate, and what I should focus on next? I'd really value your perspective.
```

**Asking for a reference**

```
I'm starting to apply for [roles] and wondered if you'd be comfortable being a reference for me. To make it easy, here's a short summary of what I worked on and the results: [3–4 bullets]. Totally understand if the timing doesn't work.
```

**Declining an offer graciously**

```
Thank you so much for the offer — it means a lot. After a lot of thought, I've decided to accept a role at [company / in another area], so I'll have to decline. I learned a huge amount from you and the team, especially [specific thing], and I'd love to stay in touch.
```

**Staying in touch, a few months later**

```
Hi [Name] — hope things are going well with [project/team]! Quick update: I'm now [what you're doing]. I've used [something you taught me] more than once already, so thank you again. Hope to cross paths again soon.
```
