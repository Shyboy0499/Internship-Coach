# Skills by Role

What to brush up on before (and during) an internship, by field.

**How to use this file:** pick the section that matches the intern's role and combine it with their job description, which is the most specific source available. Recommend a focused plan — three to five things for the weeks before they start — not everything listed here. Nobody expects an intern to arrive as an expert; the goal is less friction in week one.

The single best prep move for any intern: ask the recruiter or manager, "Is there anything you'd recommend I learn or read before I start?" It gets the most accurate answer and makes a strong first impression. (There's an email template for this in `templates.md`.)

## Everyone, in any field

- **Writing:** short, clear messages and emails that lead with the point.
- **Note-taking:** one running doc for answers, names, acronyms, commands, and decisions.
- **Spreadsheets:** sort and filter, basic formulas (SUM, AVERAGE, IF, XLOOKUP or VLOOKUP), pivot tables, a simple chart.
- **Managing your own work:** a task list, a calendar you actually use, rough time estimates.
- **Workplace tools:** the basics of Slack or Teams, Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, and whatever tracker the team uses (Jira, Asana, Linear, Notion, Confluence).
- **Presenting:** a clear five-to-ten-minute talk with a few slides.
- **Meeting basics:** arrive on time, mute when not talking, follow the team's camera norms, take notes.
- **Knowing the company:** what it sells, who its customers are, recent news, and what your team's product or function does.

## Software engineering

**Brush up on:**
- Git beyond the basics: branches, merge vs. rebase, resolving conflicts, good commit messages, the pull-request workflow.
- The team's main language and the framework named in the job description.
- Reading unfamiliar code: navigating a large codebase with search and the IDE, reading tests to understand behavior.
- Debugging: using a debugger, reading stack traces and logs.
- Testing: writing unit tests in the language's standard framework.
- Command-line basics; Docker basics if it's mentioned.
- Basic SQL.

**What impresses mentors:** small, well-described pull requests; tests included; running the test suite and self-reviewing the diff before asking for review; responding to code review without defensiveness; fixing the setup docs after hitting a snag.

**Common pitfalls:** giant pull requests; rewriting existing code into "your style"; skipping tests; submitting AI-generated code you haven't read, run, or understood; skipping the README and contribution guide; force-pushing shared branches; touching production without asking.

## Data science and analytics

**Brush up on:**
- SQL: joins, GROUP BY, window functions, CTEs.
- Dataframes (pandas or the team's equivalent) and a notebook environment.
- Statistics basics: distributions, hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, A/B testing.
- Visualization: choosing the right chart and labeling it properly.
- The BI tool the team uses (Tableau, Looker, Power BI, and so on).

**What impresses mentors:** sanity-checking data before analyzing it; stating assumptions; leading with the answer and then the evidence; reproducible notebooks; knowing which business decision the analysis feeds.

**Common pitfalls:** analyzing before understanding the business question; ignoring nulls, duplicates, and outliers; overly complex models where a simple one would do; charts without a takeaway.

## Machine learning and AI

**Brush up on:**
- Python and the team's framework (PyTorch or JAX, typically).
- Training-loop fundamentals, evaluation metrics, and train/validation/test discipline.
- Experiment tracking (Weights & Biases, MLflow, or similar).
- Reading papers efficiently, especially the team's recent ones.
- Shared GPU or cluster etiquette.

**What impresses mentors:** establishing baselines first; careful experiment logs; small-scale tests before large runs; honest reporting of negative results.

**Common pitfalls:** burning shared compute on untested large runs; not logging configs and seeds; claiming improvements without a proper evaluation; data leakage between train and test.

## Product management

**Brush up on:**
- The product itself — use it deeply, as a customer would.
- Writing a short spec or PRD.
- User-research basics: interviewing without leading questions.
- Prioritization frameworks (RICE, impact vs. effort).
- Basic analytics and SQL, and the metrics the team cares about.

**What impresses mentors:** talking to real users; clear written specs; knowing the numbers; asking engineers and designers about constraints before promising anything.

**Common pitfalls:** jumping to solutions before understanding the problem; committing engineers' time on their behalf; vague specs.

## Design (UX/UI)

**Brush up on:**
- Figma: components, auto layout, prototyping.
- The company's design system, if it's public.
- Accessibility basics: color contrast, focus states, the core WCAG ideas.
- User-research methods and usability testing.
- Presenting design rationale.

**What impresses mentors:** explaining *why* behind each decision; designing within the existing system; covering edge, empty, and error states; asking for critique early.

**Common pitfalls:** polishing too early; ignoring engineering constraints; taking critique personally.

## Marketing and communications

**Brush up on:**
- The brand's voice and recent campaigns.
- Copywriting for the channels the team uses.
- Campaign analytics basics and SEO basics.
- Canva or the Adobe tools the team uses.

**What impresses mentors:** on-brand first drafts; knowing which metric a piece of work is meant to move; quick turnaround; ideas grounded in data.

**Common pitfalls:** publishing or posting anything externally without approval; ignoring the brand guidelines.

## Finance, accounting, consulting, and business

**Brush up on:**
- Excel beyond basics: keyboard shortcuts, XLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH, pivot tables, financial functions, clean model structure.
- Reading the three financial statements.
- PowerPoint storylining: answer-first slides with action titles.
- Basics of the industry the team covers.

**What impresses mentors:** numbers that tie out; checking your own work before sending; clean formatting; strict confidentiality.

**Common pitfalls:** hard-coded numbers buried in models; unchecked arithmetic; sharing confidential material carelessly.

## Hardware, mechanical, and electrical engineering

**Brush up on:**
- The CAD or EDA tool named in the job description (SolidWorks, Fusion, Altium, KiCad, and so on).
- Reading drawings and datasheets.
- Lab equipment: multimeter, oscilloscope, power supplies.
- MATLAB or Python for test data.
- Lab safety procedures.

**What impresses mentors:** following safety procedures without being reminded; documenting every test; asking before using or changing shared equipment.

**Common pitfalls:** skipping safety training; reconfiguring a shared setup without telling anyone.

## Research (academic or industry lab)

**Brush up on:**
- Three to five of the lab's recent papers.
- The lab's core methods and tools.
- A reference manager, and LaTeX if the lab writes in it.
- Lab-notebook practice.

**What impresses mentors:** a careful lab notebook; reproducible work; arriving at meetings with results *and* questions; reading beyond what was assigned.

**Common pitfalls:** waiting to be told every next step; not recording failed experiments; messy data management.

## A role not listed here

Work from the job description: list every tool and skill it mentions, pick the three to five that seem most central to the day-to-day work, and plan focused practice on those — plus the "Everyone" section above.

## Building a pre-start learning plan

- Size it to the time actually available — a few focused hours a week for two to four weeks is plenty.
- Give each week one theme and a concrete outcome ("build a small dashboard from a public dataset" beats "study Tableau").
- Put the highest-value item first, in case the plan gets cut short.
- Leave the last few days before the start date light.
